438 College Football Teams Never Got a Market Price This Season. CrystlQuant Forecast Every One

438 college football teams went all autumn without a market price. Blindside forecast every game they played and named 1,672 winners nobody priced.

19 NOV 2026 / 4 min read

On Saturday 7 November, Alfred State played Alfred in football. The two colleges share one small village in western New York, and it came down to two points: Alfred State won 14-12. Nobody put a price on it. There was no line on who would win, no line on the margin, nothing. Blindside had a number anyway. It took Alfred State at 51.4%, written down that morning, hours before kickoff.

An hour later and a long way south, Furman won 24-14 at The Citadel. That one is a Division I game between two old Southern schools, and the market did not price it either. Blindside took Furman at 51.6%. Two games nobody priced, two winners named.

Neither game will make anyone's highlights. Together they are the clearest picture we have of what CrystlQuant is as a business.

Most of college football has no price

That weekend, 7 and 8 November, the market had a closing price on the winner for 55 college football games. Another 258 went off with no price at all, and Blindside named the winner in 200 of them.

That was not a strange weekend. It is the shape of the whole season. Between 10 September and 8 November, our tables hold 695 college football teams. Only 257 of them played even one game with a market line of any kind. The other 438 went the entire autumn without a single price on their names: hundreds of teams, from Division III colleges with a few thousand students to Division I schools like The Citadel, whose games simply never got a number.

Americans tend to picture college football as the hundred-odd big schools on Saturday television. It is much wider than that. Below the top level sit several more divisions and an entire second association of small colleges, and every one of those teams plays a full schedule in front of students, parents, alumni and a town that cares. Most of them play in front of no market at all.

Why the market stops where it does

A market price is a promise. Whoever posts it is offering to take money on both sides, and if the number is off, someone who knows better takes it from them. That is why the price on a big game gets so much attention: a lot of money pushes it hard.

On a game like Alfred State at Alfred the sums run the other way. Very few people want in, so there is little to earn, and the people who do want in are the ones most likely to know something: the coach's cousin, the team's own students. Getting that number right is hard work, and the reward for the work is small. So the market leaves it alone.

A model faces a different sum. Once it knows how to read a football team from the teams and players themselves, a game in Alfred is no harder to read than a game in Ann Arbor. The market's job ends where the money ends. A model's job ends where the schedule ends.

What Blindside did on its own

That is why CrystlQuant runs two numbers. They say nobody beats the market. One model never sees the market price. The other fixes it. The second one, CrystlQuant's main number, starts from the price and moves it toward what we see, so it can only work where a price exists: it covered 395 college football games this season. Blindside covered 2,704.

Of those 2,704, the market had a closing price for 518. That leaves 2,186 games, between 10 September and 8 November, where Blindside was the only number anyone had. It named 1,672 winners in them.

The count is only half of it, because a lot of those games are lopsided. The other half is that the model knew which ones. When Blindside was 90% sure or more, its side won 640 times. And when it was barely leaning, as it was on Alfred State and Furman, it still had to name a side, and on both of those Saturdays it named the winner. No price to lean on, no crowd to follow, just the teams.

Why that is the business

CrystlQuant is free and carries ads. A site like that lives on two things: people who come back because last week's numbers came in, and pages nobody else has. The big Saturday games will always have a hundred places offering a view. A game between two colleges in the same New York village has, as far as the market goes, none.

That is where an audience is cheapest to win and hardest to copy. Every Saturday this autumn, a couple of hundred games went off with no price, and Blindside had a number on every one of them, out before kickoff. A site starting next year cannot go back and fill in this season. Big games are where we stand next to the market. Small games are where we are often the only number there is, and the 438 teams nobody priced are the clearest proof that the second half of the business is real.
